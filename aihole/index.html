Karnataka Trade Cities

The Unavoidable Workshop

Aihole, Badami, and the making of a trading empire — how a temple town three hundred kilometres from the sea helped build a merchant network that would one day reach Sumatra and China.

— Ram Devagiri
I.

A poet returns to finish a temple

In 634 CE, a Jain poet named Ravikirti climbed a low hill above the town of Aihole to finish a building he had started years earlier. He was not a king, and the temple he was completing — the Meguti, a modest structure by the standards of what Aihole would eventually produce — was not commissioned by one either. It was his own. What he carved into its eastern wall, in nineteen lines of ornamented Sanskrit, was not a prayer. It was a résumé: a genealogy of the dynasty he served, a catalogue of his patron's victories, and, in its closing boast, a claim that his own poetry deserved comparison with Kalidasa's. Ravikirti was not being falsely modest, and he was not wrong to be confident. His inscription would outlive the empire it celebrated by well over a thousand years, and it remains, to this day, the single most important document historians have for reconstructing the dynasty that built it.[1]

Read start to finish, the nineteen lines run through a fairly consistent shape. It opens with a Jain devotional salutation to Jinendra, then praises Pulakeshin II for his generosity before tracing the dynasty's line back through Jayasimha-vallabha and his son Ranaraga — the same ancestors this account has already used to open the Chalukya story. The historical core follows: Pulakeshin's defeat of Harshavardhana, his victory over the Pallavas, and the shift of the capital from Aihole to Badami. One verse is a genuine oddity among Indian inscriptions — it dates itself not by a regnal year but by counting forward from the Mahabharata war, placing the inscription 3,735 years after that mythic conflict. It closes with Ravikirti naming himself both the eulogy's author and the temple's founder, and staking his own claim to literary greatness alongside Kalidasa.[1b]

That dynasty was the Chalukyas of Badami, and by the time Ravikirti picked up his chisel, they had already spent two generations turning a stretch of the Malaprabha river valley into something without real precedent on the subcontinent: a place where architecture itself became a form of accumulated wealth, where more than a hundred temples would eventually rise within a few square kilometres, tried out in sixteen different structural forms before anyone agreed on which ones worked. Aihole was not a capital when this began. It was, more than anything else, a workshop — and the question worth asking, the same question we've previously asked of Banavasi, is not simply who ruled here, but what made a workshop like this possible, and who paid for it.

This is a story that starts, as the last one did, with people who were not kings — and it ends, four centuries later, with the founding of an organisation of merchants who would eventually eclipse the dynasty that made their rise possible.

II.

The officials who became kings

The Chalukyas did not arrive in the Malaprabha valley as conquerors from outside. Mayurasharma, founder of the Kadambas at Banavasi, was an outsider — a scholar turned rebel, forcing recognition from a power that had humiliated him. The Chalukyas' rise looks almost nothing like that. According to the Aihole inscription itself, the dynasty's earliest traceable ancestors, a certain Jayasimha and his successor Ranaraga, were not rebels at all. They were administrative officers serving the Kadambas of Banavasi, in the Badami region itself — men who worked inside the system for a generation or more before their descendants broke away from it.[2]

It was Ranaraga's son, Pulakesin I, who converted that inherited administrative footing into sovereignty. Around 543 CE, he fortified a low hill above a town called Vatapi — Badami, in its modern name — and performed an Ashvamedha, the classical Vedic horse sacrifice by which a king formally declared himself subordinate to no one.[3] It was a calculated piece of political theatre: a former subordinate's son, staging the most maximalist ritual claim to independence available in the Sanskrit political vocabulary, on a hill his own family had probably once administered on someone else's behalf.

His son Kirtivarman I, who ruled from 566 to 597, did the work of turning that declaration into a real kingdom. He is credited with conquering the Nalas of Bastar, the Mauryas of the Konkan coast, and — the detail that ties this story directly to the one before it — the Kadambas of Banavasi themselves, the very dynasty his own family had once served.[4] It is a genuinely striking inversion: within two generations, the Chalukyas had gone from Kadamba officials to Kadamba conquerors, absorbing the town whose administrative machinery had trained the family that ended it.

What followed was messier. Kirtivarman I's brother, Mangalesa, took the throne as regent for Kirtivarman's young son — and then declined to give it back. He ruled in his own right from 597 to 609, defeating the Kalachuris of western India in the process, but his refusal to step aside for his nephew eventually cost him everything: he was defeated and killed by that same nephew, who took the throne as Pulakeshin II.[5]

Pulakeshin II, who reigned from 609 to 642, is the figure the entire dynasty is remembered through, and Ravikirti's inscription is substantially his biography. Two moments from his reign matter most for a history built around trade rather than conquest for its own sake.

The first is his war with Harshavardhana, the great north Indian emperor, which culminated in a battle on the banks of the Narmada around 630 CE. Pulakeshin won, and the Aihole inscription credits him with the title Parameshvara — supreme lord — for having done what no other southern king had managed: stopping Harsha's expansion cold.[6] The reasons he could do this are, for our purposes, more interesting than the victory itself. Harsha's army, drawing on the plains of the Gangetic north, had access to something the Deccan simply did not: the overland horse-trade routes running down from Central Asia, feeding cavalry into every north Indian army at a scale the Deccan could never match on land alone.[7] The Chalukyas fielded a real cavalry force regardless — which means, by necessity, that Chalukya warhorses were arriving another way. They were coming by sea, through the same western coastline and the same trading networks that moved pepper, gems, and Roman coin through Banavasi a few centuries earlier. Pulakeshin's capacity to fight a northern empire on equal terms was not simply a matter of generalship. It was underwritten, structurally, by a maritime trade network most histories of this war never mention.

The second moment is smaller, stranger, and just as revealing. Around 625–626 CE, Pulakeshin II sent an embassy to the Sasanian Persian court of Khusrau II — and this embassy is not just a line in an inscription. It survives as an image: a painting at Ajanta, in Cave 1, believed to depict the Persian king receiving the Chalukya envoys.[8] It is a rare thing — a diplomatic act from this exact period that we can, quite literally, look at. Pulakeshin was not merely fighting wars. He was building relationships with the western end of the very trade network his cavalry depended on.

None of it lasted for him personally. In 642 CE, the Pallava king Narasimhavarman I — avenging his own father's earlier defeat — invaded the Deccan, defeated Pulakeshin II, and captured Vatapi itself.[9] Pulakeshin is not recorded as surviving the war. His son Vikramaditya I spent the following decade clawing the kingdom back, retaking Badami and restoring Chalukya authority by around 655 CE — a reminder that in this period, even the most successful dynasty's grip on its own capital was never permanent.[10]

The defeat left a smaller, stranger trace as well, one that has nothing to do with armies or capitals. Vatapi is also the name of a god — a form of Ganesha, worshipped in a small temple on the hill overlooking Badami itself. Local tradition holds that when Narasimhavarman I's forces took the city, they carried the deity's image away with them as a spoil of the Pallava victory.[10b] Whether or not the claim survives close historical scrutiny, it captures something true about how thoroughly this defeat registered: not merely a captured fort or a dead king, but, in local memory, a captured god, carried south with everything else the Pallavas judged worth taking.

III.

Three sites, three jobs

It is easy, standing at Aihole, Badami, and Pattadakal today, to think of them as one undifferentiated cluster of "Chalukya temples." They are usually visited in a single circuit, and the modern drive between them takes under an hour. But the Chalukyas themselves used these three places for three distinct purposes, and understanding that division explains almost everything about how — and why — over a hundred temples came to exist within a few square kilometres of river valley.

Aihole was the workshop. It was already a settled town before the Chalukyas built anything there — with existing masons, existing sandstone quarries, and no particular ceremonial weight attached to it. That combination of low stakes and available skilled labour is precisely what made it the site of genuine architectural experimentation: sixteen distinct forms of free-standing temple, four types of rock-cut shrine, tried out over roughly two centuries without the pressure of getting it right the first time.[11] Nowhere else in India were builders working in such openly divergent styles in such close proximity — a fact one architectural historian attributes directly to the Chalukyas' geographic position, wedged between the Nagara traditions of the north and the Dravida traditions of the south, with craftsmen imported from as far as Kanchipuram working alongside local Deccan masons.[12]

Badami was the fortress-capital. Pulakeshin I's choice of Vatapi had nothing to do with commerce and everything to do with defensibility: a natural cleft between two sandstone cliffs, a lake fed between them (later formalised into the Agastya lake by the 7th century), and a position that could be fortified and held.[13] Its cave temples — rock-cut rather than free-standing, carved directly into the cliff face — reflect this: they are a capital's monuments, built to last through siege and to declare permanence, not a workshop's experiments.

Pattadakal was the ceremonial stage. The name likely derives from pattada-kisuvolal, "the town of the coronation," and it functioned as the site where kings were formally consecrated and where state ritual played out.[14] It sits on the same river, drawing on the same sandstone and often the same craftsmen as Aihole, but the architecture here is not experimental — it is the mature, confident synthesis of everything Aihole had tried. This is where the Vesara style, the deliberate fusion of northern Nagara and southern Dravida temple forms, reaches its fullest expression, most famously in the Virupaksha temple, built to commemorate a Chalukya victory over the Pallavas and — in one of history's better ironies — later serving as the direct architectural model for the Kailasa temple at Ellora, commissioned by the very Rashtrakuta dynasty that would end Chalukya rule.[15]

The coronation itself, pattabhisheka, had a physical anchor: a specific rock at the point where the Malaprabha does something no other stretch of the river does, briefly reversing to flow north rather than south — uttaravahini, a direction considered auspicious, facing toward Kailasha. Crown princes were consecrated on that rock, not merely in the town generally, and at least one such coronation survives by name: Vinayaditya, anointed there in the late 7th century.[63] Pattadakal's temples, in other words, were not simply built near a coronation site. Several of them stand on the site itself.

The Virupaksha and Mallikarjuna temples add a second layer worth pulling apart, because they were not, strictly, royal commissions at all. Both were funded by Lokamahadevi and Trailokyamahadevi — sisters from the Kalachuri dynasty, married to the same king, Vikramaditya II — and both drew on a specific, separate channel of money: stridhana, a woman's independently held wealth, legally distinct from both her husband's and the state's treasury under Dharmashastra and Arthashastra convention, built originally from bridal gifts and expandable, by this period, into land, cash, and cattle in her own name.[64] One inscription states plainly that Lokamahadevi financed her temple's architects "using her independent royal treasury" — not the crown's revenue, not her husband's. The architect she paid is himself named: Gundan Anivaritachari, titled Tenkanadesiya Sutradhari, "master builder of the southern country."[65] The temples are usually read as twin acts of wifely devotion, commemorating the same victory. The stone complicates that reading: Lokamahadevi's own inscription describes her, not her husband, as the one "who three times conquered Kanchi" — and historians now increasingly read the two temples as evidence the sisters were competing with each other, each funding her own monument from her own money, rather than jointly celebrating his.[66] The money that built Pattadakal's grandest temple, in short, never touched the tax ledgers this book has spent so much time tracing. It ran on an entirely separate rail — a queen's own account, filled by marriage and inheritance rather than Herjunka or Bilkode.

The closest thing to a documented victory ceremony in this account comes not from a coronation but from the aftermath of conquest itself, and it says something real about how success was marked. When Vikramaditya II took Kanchipuram — the third time his family had done so — his own inscription records a deliberate, almost ritualised sequence: he entered the city without destroying it, visited the Pallavas' Rajasimhesvara temple, made "lavish donations of gold and jewels" to it, ordered the city's own treasures returned rather than carried off, and distributed gifts to Brahmins and to the poor.[67] He then had a Kannada inscription cut into a pillar inside that same temple — a permanent record of clemency displayed, deliberately, inside the loser's own sacred space, the same gesture this book has already noted his ancestors made with their own Kannada graffiti at Kanchi generations earlier. Victory, in other words, was not celebrated with a feast this record preserves. It was celebrated with temple donations, redistributed wealth, and an inscription — success converted, immediately and visibly, into religious merit.

Three sites, three functions: one for trying things, one for holding ground, one for staging power. None of them makes complete sense without the other two.

It is worth stopping here, before moving on, to sit with exactly how remote a spot Aihole actually is — because the distance is the whole point, and the same argument that mattered so much for Banavasi applies here with even more force. Aihole sits at roughly 16.02°N, 75.88°E, some 810 metres above sea level, in what is now Bagalkot district.[15b] The nearest real stretch of Arabian Sea coastline, near Gokarna, lies something like 300 kilometres away by the best modern road — a distance that, whatever the exact ancient route, meant days of overland travel through the Western Ghats before a single bale of goods reached tidewater.[15c] That is more than three times the roughly eighty kilometres separating Banavasi from its own coastal outlet. Aihole is not merely inland. It is inland in a way that makes Banavasi look almost coastal by comparison.

There is a second, sharper piece of geography that makes the point even more starkly. The Malaprabha, the river Aihole sits beside, does not flow toward the Arabian Sea at all. It is a tributary of the Krishna, and the Krishna crosses the entire breadth of the Deccan Plateau to empty into the Bay of Bengal, on India's eastern coast, hundreds of kilometres in the opposite direction.[15d] Banavasi's river, the Varada, at least ran roughly toward the western Ghat passes it needed. Aihole's river runs the wrong way entirely. Anything moving between Aihole and the Arabian Sea trade world had to leave the river system altogether and cross open country and mountain passes on pack animal and cart — there was no floating a single log of goods downstream toward the ocean we keep returning to.

And yet the goods moved anyway, and by the eighth century, so did an entire merchant institution capable of running a trade network reaching Sumatra and China. The textile record makes this concrete in a way abstract trade-route diagrams cannot. In the same Bagalkot district that contains Aihole itself sits the town of Ilkal, whose signature weaving tradition — the Ilkal saree, still produced and GI-protected today — textile historians trace specifically to the eighth century and the Chalukya dynasty.[15e] Its defining technique, tope teni (sometimes rendered kondi), involves weaving the body of the garment and its decorative pallu as two entirely separate pieces on the loom, in different yarns, then interlocking them seamlessly by hand — cotton, grown locally in the region's black soil, for the body; silk, which this landlocked plateau did not itself produce in any quantity, for the pallu and borders.[15f] A saree woven three hundred kilometres from the nearest coast, in a workshop town whose river flowed the wrong way, nonetheless required thread that had travelled a genuinely long distance to get there — silk moving inland against the same geography that made pepper and horses move outward. This is, in miniature, the whole argument: Aihole did not need to sit on the coast, or even face the right way, to be plugged into a trade network spanning the Arabian Sea and beyond. It needed only skilled hands, a demand for what those hands could make, and merchants organised enough to move raw silk in and finished cloth out across three hundred kilometres of hostile terrain, reliably, for centuries.

That organisation, when it eventually put a name to itself, called itself after this exact town. The Ayyavole 500 — Ayyavole simply being Aihole's own Kannada name — would go on to build precisely the kind of trade empire this remoteness seems to rule out: private fleets, fortified trading colonies as far as Sumatra, a documented presence in Song-dynasty China. That story belongs properly to a later part of this series. But it is worth planting the flag here, at the geography, before the politics and the architecture take over the narrative again: whatever the Ayyavole 500 eventually became, they built it starting from one of the least maritime-looking places imaginable — a temple town on a river that flows toward the wrong ocean, three hundred kilometres from the nearest port, weaving silk it had never grown and would never see arrive.

IV.

What the stones prove

The obvious question — asked before, of Banavasi's coin hoards — is what all this architecture actually proves about wealth. A temple is not a balance sheet. No construction budgets survive from sixth-century Karnataka, no wage records for the masons who cut sandstone at Aihole for two centuries. What survives is the stone itself, and it is worth taking seriously as economic evidence in its own right, not merely as art history.

Start with scale. Over one hundred and twenty temples at Aihole alone, more again at Pattadakal and Badami, sustained not across a single reign but across roughly two hundred years — spanning multiple kings, at least one dynastic near-collapse, and a foreign occupation of the capital itself.[16] Building on this scale, for this long, through this much political disruption, is not something a single ruler's patronage can explain. It points instead toward sustained local prosperity: an economy capable of funding continuous religious construction as a kind of background hum, independent of whoever currently held Vatapi.

Then there is the labour itself. Sculptors were deliberately brought in from Kanchipuram — the Pallava capital, the Chalukyas' great rival — to work at Aihole and Pattadakal.[17] Importing skilled craftsmen from a rival kingdom's own capital is not a cheap or simple transaction; it implies both the wealth to pay for it and a functioning network of contacts and safe transit capable of moving specialists across a contested frontier in wartime.

And there is the navy. Pulakeshin II is credited with a fleet of roughly one hundred ships, sufficient to take Revatidvipa — Goa — on the western coast and to project power as far as Puri on the eastern one.[18] A navy of that size is not free, and it is not primarily a military asset in the narrow sense: the same ships, the same crews, the same coastal infrastructure that let the Chalukyas take Goa are the ships, crews, and infrastructure that could move goods, and that almost certainly did, alongside soldiers.

None of this is direct proof of a merchant class in the way Banavasi's Roman coin moulds or Bhutapala's inscription at Karla were direct proof. That is a real limitation of this period's evidence, and it is worth being honest about rather than papering over: we have not yet found this era's Bhutapala — a named individual merchant whose wealth we can point to the way we could at Banavasi. What we have instead is a slower, more structural kind of evidence — stone, imported skill, and naval reach — that points toward a trading economy operating in the background of every battle and every temple, without yet putting a face to it. That face does not arrive until the very end of this part of the story.

V.

The pilgrim who had already met the loser

There is one witness to all of this who owed the Chalukyas nothing, and it is worth spending real time on him, because everything else so far — Ravikirti's inscription, the Aihole eulogy, the temple walls themselves — was produced by people paid, one way or another, to make Pulakeshin II look good. The one figure who was not is a Chinese Buddhist monk named Xuanzang, and the strange, roundabout way he ended up in Vatapi is itself worth telling.

Xuanzang had left China in 629 CE, alone and without official permission, on a journey that would take the better part of two decades: west across the Taklamakan desert, through the Central Asian oasis cities, over the Hindu Kush, and down into India, all in pursuit of authentic Buddhist scripture and the teachers who could explain it to him.[19] By the time he reached the Indian subcontinent he had already survived sandstorms, bandits, and at least one occasion on which he nearly died of thirst in the desert. He studied for years at Nalanda, then the greatest Buddhist university in the world, and travelled the length and breadth of India collecting manuscripts, debating scholars, and recording — in obsessive, list-making detail — everything he saw.

Before any of that, though, he had spent time as a guest at the court of Harshavardhana, the north Indian emperor — the very man Pulakeshin II would go on to defeat at the Narmada. Harsha, by Xuanzang's own account, treated him with enormous favour, staging vast public debates and religious assemblies in his honour.[20] This matters for how we should read what comes next. When Xuanzang arrived in the Deccan in 641–642 CE, on his long return journey home, he was not a stranger passing blind through unfamiliar territory. He was, in effect, the personal guest of the loser, now walking into the winner's capital.

What he wrote about Vatapi is some of the most vivid outside description we have of any city in this entire history. He recorded it as a fortified city roughly eight miles in circumference, tucked among mountains and river valleys, its surrounding land fertile enough to produce grain, sugarcane, and fruit in abundance.[21] He counted, or was told, that the city held around a hundred Buddhist monasteries, home to some five thousand monks practising both the Mahayana and the older Hinayana traditions side by side — a detail that becomes genuinely striking once you remember that Pulakeshin II himself was a devoted worshipper of Shiva, not Buddha. Xuanzang noted this without complaint, and seemingly with real approval: a Shaivite king, presiding over a capital thick with well-funded Buddhist institutions, extending patronage across sectarian lines rather than favouring only his own. It is independent, foreign, dateable confirmation of a kind of religious pluralism that shows up again and again in the physical record at Aihole itself, where Hindu, Jain, and Buddhist structures were raised within sight of one another.

He described the king — whose name he renders as "Pu-lo-ki-she," a Chinese ear's best attempt at Pulakeshin — as a man "of farsighted resource and astuteness, who extends kindness to all," ruling a population that obeyed him "with perfect submission."[22] His subjects, in Xuanzang's telling, were tall, dark-complexioned, proud, and fiercely martial, "honest" in manner but "hasty," rough enough in their bearing that even a well-travelled monk who had crossed half of Asia thought it worth remarking on. And crucially, on the single fact that mattered most to Pulakeshin's own propaganda machine, Xuanzang's account lines up exactly with the Aihole inscription: Harsha, he confirms, marched south against the Chalukya king in full confidence of victory, and was unable to subdue him.[23] Two entirely independent sources — one carved in stone by a court poet paid to flatter his patron, the other written from memory in China years later by a monk with nothing to gain from the comparison — agree on the central fact of Pulakeshin's reign. That kind of corroboration is rare in this period, and it is worth treating as exactly as valuable as it is.

But there is a wider point buried in Xuanzang's presence at Vatapi at all, and it is the point most relevant to a history built around trade rather than kings. A Chinese monk, travelling on foot and on borrowed transport across the entire breadth of Asia, in pursuit of religious learning, ending up as a guest first of a north Indian emperor and then of a Deccan one, is not simply a story about piety. It is a demonstration, in a single human life, of how thoroughly connected this world already was — a network of roads, way-stations, monasteries, and mutual intelligibility stretching from Tang China to the Sasanian frontier, through which a lone traveller with no army and no cargo could move for the better part of two decades and be received, everywhere he went, as an intelligible and welcome guest. The same infrastructure of connection that let Persian ambassadors reach Pulakeshin's court in 625 CE, that let warhorses arrive by sea because the overland routes ran through country the Chalukyas didn't control, that let sculptors travel from Kanchipuram to Aihole in wartime — that same connective tissue is what carried a penniless, official-permission-lacking monk from the Yellow River to the Malaprabha valley and back again. Xuanzang did not come for trade. But the roads, the safe passage, the mutual recognition across kingdoms and religions that made his journey survivable at all were the same roads, the same passage, the same recognition that made trade itself possible. He is proof, walking on two feet, of a world already wired together.

VI.

Ledgers and lances

Every kingdom in this history has, at bottom, been a machine for turning trade, agriculture, and conquest into revenue, and then turning that revenue back into armies, temples, and roads capable of protecting and extending the flow of the first three. The Chalukyas built one of the more sophisticated versions of that machine that the early medieval Deccan produced, and it is worth walking through it in real detail — not as an abstraction, but as an actual chain of people, offices, and decisions, because that chain is where merchants stop being background colour and start appearing, by name, inside the government itself.

The administrative hierarchy ran in five tiers. At the top sat the Maharashtraka, a great province — at the empire's height under Pulakeshin II, there were three of them, and Xuanzang himself was told that each contained ninety-nine thousand villages, a figure so large it reads less like a precise census than a way of saying more villages than anyone could reasonably count.[24] Below the Maharashtraka came the Rashtraka, or Mandala; below that the Vishaya, a district roughly equivalent to a modern one, governed by a Vishayapati; below that the Bhoga, a cluster of about ten villages under a Bhogapati; and at the base, the Grama, the single village, overseen by a Gramabhogika.[25] Revenue matters at the top of this pyramid were the responsibility of officials called Amatyas — ministers, in effect, though the word covers a wider range of court functionaries than that English translation suggests.

The actual mechanics of collection began with measurement. Before the state could tax land, it had to know what the land produced, and Chalukya administration is recorded as having conducted land surveys specifically to establish this, assessing yield capacity village by village.[26] Only once that assessment existed could the standard rate apply: one-sixth of agricultural produce, following the old Manusmriti convention — a figure that sounds modest until you remember it was extracted from a base of ninety-nine thousand villages per province, three provinces deep, year after year.[27] The state also insisted on standardised weights and measures across its territory, without which a one-sixth assessment in one village could mean something entirely different from a one-sixth assessment in the next, and it funded irrigation canals to directly increase the yield it would later tax — investment and extraction running on the same ledger.[28]

Land tax was never the whole story. Layered on top of it was a set of explicitly commercial levies: Herjunka, a load or transit tax on goods physically in motion; Kirukula, a tax on retail goods passing through in transit; Bilkode, a sales tax; and Pannaya, a specific tax on betel leaf.[29] These are, in almost every case, the same categories of tax the Kadambas levied at Banavasi a century earlier, under slightly different names (Perjunka, Bilkoda, Kirukula, Pannaya). The Chalukyas did not reinvent Deccan taxation when they took Banavasi and absorbed its administrative territory. They inherited it, largely intact, terminology and all — one more piece of evidence, alongside Jayasimha and Ranaraga's earlier careers as Kadamba officials, that this dynasty's rise was built on continuity with the system it replaced rather than a clean break from it.

It is worth walking one commodity through this machine in full, because the categories above stay abstract until a number moves through them. Take a village producing paddy. The land survey establishes its yield; the assessment, at the standard one-sixth rate, converts a portion of that grain into taxable value.[27b] Say the village's harvest that year is worth, in the standardised measures the state itself enforced, one hundred units of grain. Sixteen and two-thirds of those units — one-sixth — are owed. The village's Gramabhogika collects it, in kind or in its assessed equivalent. That single village's sixteen-odd units are then folded into the returns of the nine or so other villages making up its Bhoga, aggregated by the Bhogapati into a district-level total. At the Vishaya level, that total is recorded and reconciled by the Vishayapati — with the chief merchant, chief banker, and guild-head of the mahallakas council in the room, since any portion of that village's crop that moved onward as a traded commodity, rather than being consumed locally, would additionally have picked up Herjunka as it left the district and Bilkode if it was sold rather than simply moved. A single measure of paddy could, in principle, be taxed once as agricultural yield and again as a commercial good in transit — two different levies, two different officials, on the same physical grain. From the Vishaya, the aggregated total climbs to the Rashtraka, then to the Maharashtraka, until it reaches the Amatyas managing revenue at the centre. Multiply that single village's sixteen-odd units by the ninety-nine thousand villages Xuanzang was told made up a single province, and the scale of what climbed this ladder every season becomes clear — even before accounting for the merchants' own commercial taxes layered on top.[27c] This is the money that built roads, paid the Chaturangini's soldiers, and funded a king's own great works. It is worth remembering, though — as Pattadakal's own temples already showed — that this was only one of two channels running through this world. This one was the crown's. A queen's ran on entirely different rails.

Here is where the merchants enter the machine directly, rather than as its taxable subjects. At district level, self-governance did not rest with the Vishayapati alone. It was shared with a council of leading local men, and the sources are explicit about who sat on it: the chief merchant, the chief banker, and the head of the artisans' guild — collectively termed mahallakas — who held, in the words of one administrative account, "a large voice in district administration" and assisted the Vishayapati whenever called upon.[30] This is not a case of merchants lobbying government from outside, the way a modern chamber of commerce might petition a council. This is merchants and bankers sitting inside the formal structure of district government, as a matter of institutional design, at the same table as the state's own revenue officer. The tax categories aimed at trade — Herjunka, Kirukula, Bilkode — were therefore not simply imposed on merchants from above. They were, at least in part, negotiated with men who were themselves merchants, sitting in the room where the district's business was actually done.

Occasionally the record lets us see this machine operating on a single, specific village, rather than as an abstract system. In 2025, archaeologists working near Madapura Lake in Davangere district uncovered a seventeen-line Old Kannada inscription, five feet long, dated to the reign of Vikramaditya I.[31] It names a local officer, Singhavenna, who — in a genuinely rare instance of recorded relief rather than recorded extraction — enacted a tax waiver for the villagers under his jurisdiction, and oversaw the donation of six acres of land for the construction of the lake itself. It is a small thing: one officer, one lake, one waiver, in a kingdom that stretched from the Kaveri to the Narmada. But it is also the only moment in this entire administrative structure where we can watch a named individual, below the rank of king, actually make a decision about tax — and the decision he made was to give some of it back.

The army this whole machine funded was organised on a classical fourfold model, the Chaturangini — infantry, cavalry, elephant corps, and chariots — commanded by a Dandanayaka (general) and, above him in the largest campaigns, a Mahaprachanda Dandanayaka.[32] Feudatory lords, the Samantas, maintained supplementary forces of their own, called up as needed rather than kept permanently under central command. Xuanzang, for his part, recorded something that reads almost like battlefield theatre: hundreds of war elephants, deliberately intoxicated with liquor before combat, sent forward as living engines of terror and disruption.[33]

The detail that matters most for a history about trade is the one already raised in the account of Pulakeshin's war with Harsha: this army's cavalry arm could not have existed on domestic breeding and overland supply alone. The great horse-trade routes of this era ran overland from Central Asia into the Gangetic plains — Harsha's territory, not the Chalukyas'. A Deccan kingdom fielding cavalry at all was, by geographic necessity, importing warhorses by sea, through the same western coastline, the same ports, and very likely some of the same merchant networks that moved pepper and gems.[34] The taxation machine described above, and the military machine it funded, were not two separate systems running in parallel. The second was quietly dependent on the trade the first was busy taxing — a closed loop in which commerce paid for the army that then protected the roads commerce needed to survive.

VII.

The ships nobody carved

Every claim made so far about sea-borne cavalry raises an obvious question this account has so far dodged: what did the actual ships look like? A horse is not a sack of pepper. It is a large, panicking, four-legged animal that eats and drinks continuously, cannot swim any real distance, and dies easily in rough weather or bad handling — and yet enough of them survived enough crossings, reliably enough, to keep a Deccan cavalry force in the field for generations. That is a genuine feat of maritime engineering and animal husbandry, and it deserves its own accounting rather than a passing mention.

The honest starting point is a gap. No temple wall at Aihole, Badami, or Pattadakal carves a ship — this is a civilisation that put extraordinary effort into recording gods, kings, dancers, and donors in stone, and left no equivalent image of the vessels its own cavalry depended on. What survives instead is the broader Indian Ocean shipbuilding tradition these vessels almost certainly belonged to, which is well enough documented, across a wide enough span of centuries either side of the Chalukyas, to reconstruct with real confidence even without a single Chalukya-specific hull.

Construction was stitched, not nailed. Indian Ocean vessels of this era — what later sources call dhows — were built from planks sewn together with coir rope made from coconut husk, the seams packed with oil-soaked cotton to swell and seal on contact with water; iron nails were avoided almost universally across this tradition, for practical reasons (rust in salt water) as much as the folk beliefs later texts record.[34b] The sixth-century Byzantine historian Procopius, describing Indian Ocean vessels in terms that match this construction precisely, wrote that they were "not covered with pitch or any substance," their planks "fastened together, not with nails but with cords" — and by the eighth century itself, fleets built this way were already, in one historian's phrase, part of "a commercial maritime network not matched or superseded until the European circumnavigation of the globe."[34c] This was not a primitive technology limping toward something better. It was a mature, deliberately chosen system that would go on serving the Indian Ocean trade for another thousand years with only gradual change.

Where they were built is a question with a real, if partial, answer. The Malabar coast — modern Kerala — was one of the great shipbuilding centres of this entire tradition, and for a specific, practical reason: the forests around what is now Beypore produced exceptional teak, coir was locally abundant, and generations of shipwrights had turned hull-building into hereditary, closely guarded craft knowledge.[34d] Arab traders — Omani and Yemeni sailors above all — travelled specifically to this coast to have their vessels built, rather than shipping the expertise the other way; the large teak-keeled dhow still built at Beypore today, the uru, is a direct descendant of exactly this arrangement. It means the ships that carried Chalukya warhorses were very plausibly Indian-built even when Arab-crewed or Arab-owned — the line between "whose trade" and "whose ship" blurring in exactly the way it blurs everywhere else in this account.

Sails were lateen or settee-rigged — the long, triangular sail associated with the whole dhow family, efficient at sailing closer to the wind than a square rig, and essential for working the monsoon's shifting angles rather than simply running before it. Navigation depended on that same monsoon system already described in Banavasi's own story: outbound in one half of the year, return in the other, with pilots reading stars, coastline, and the wind's seasonal turn rather than any instrument.[34e]

For the physical experience of loading and carrying horses specifically, the best surviving direct description comes centuries later than the Chalukyas, and it is worth being explicit about that gap rather than smoothing over it: Marco Polo, describing Indian Ocean horse-transport vessels in the thirteenth century, wrote that they had "one mast, one sail, one helm," were undecked, and that once loaded, the merchants "cover the cargo with hides, and on top of these they place the horses."[34f] A Timurid envoy to Calicut two centuries after that, Abd al-Razzaq, records nearly losing consciousness from the smell the moment he boarded one of these ships.[34g] Neither writer is describing a Chalukya-era vessel. But the underlying technology — stitched hulls, single mast, lateen sail, no deck — changed slowly enough across this tradition that both accounts almost certainly describe, in its essentials, the same kind of ship that carried horses to the Chalukyas nine hundred years earlier. The alternative — assuming the basic method was reinvented from scratch sometime between the seventh century and the thirteenth — has no evidence behind it at all.

What this arrangement meant in practice was brutal. Horses stood undecked, on a bed of hides laid over the cargo hold, exposed to weather and to each other, for a crossing that could run to weeks depending on wind and route. Not all of them survived it — later customs records from Goa (1486) price surviving Arabian horses at 100 to 150 gold pardaos each, a sum that fed a family for roughly two years, and that price already accounts for the ones that did not make it.[34h] Embarkation and disembarkation almost certainly meant walking animals through shallow water to small boats or a beach landing rather than any pier or dock — early medieval Indian ports rarely had deep-water quays capable of berthing a ship directly, so cargo of every kind, horses included, made the last stretch of any voyage on foot or by hand.

None of this can be pinned to a named ship, a named captain, or a named port serving Vatapi specifically, and that gap should stand alongside the missing Chalukya-era Bhutapala as an honest limit of what this evidence can prove. But the shape of the answer is clear enough: stitched hulls built from Malabar teak and coconut coir, very possibly at yards near Beypore or elsewhere on the same coast; single-masted, lateen-rigged, undecked; horses loaded onto hides and carried exposed for a monsoon-dependent crossing that killed a meaningful fraction of its cargo before it ever reached a Deccan saddle. Every cavalry charge the Aihole inscription boasts about had, somewhere behind it, an animal that had already survived the worst part of the journey.

VIII.

The conquest that closes a circle

It is worth returning, now, to a single sentence from earlier and sitting with it properly, because it deserves more than the passing mention it got. Between 566 and 597 CE, Kirtivarman I — Pulakeshin I's son, the second king of an independent Chalukya line barely one generation old — defeated the Kadambas of Banavasi.[35] This is not a footnote. It is the hinge on which the first and second parts of this series become, formally, one continuous story rather than two adjacent ones.

Consider what Kirtivarman was actually doing. His own father, and his father's father before him, had served as administrative officers inside the Kadamba system — men who understood Banavasi's customs posts, its Ghat-pass tolls, its coinage, its guild structures, from the inside, because they had once helped run them. When Kirtivarman marched against Banavasi, he was not discovering unfamiliar territory. He was taking something his family already knew how to operate, from people his family had once answered to. It is difficult to think of a cleaner illustration of how power actually changed hands in the early medieval Deccan: not through conquest by total strangers, but through the slow, patient climb of a provincial administrative family, followed by one sharp, decisive break at the moment that family judged itself strong enough to stop taking orders.

The Kadambas did not vanish. Individual Kadamba lineages persisted for centuries afterward as feudatories in Goa, Hangal, and Halasi, demoted from sovereigns to subordinates but never fully erased.[36] What Kirtivarman actually annexed, in practical terms, was not simply a royal title but an entire working apparatus — the customs officials who taxed goods moving through the Western Ghats passes, the coinage tradition running back through the Kadamba Padmatanka to Satavahana lead issues and Roman bullion, the trade-tax categories (Perjunka, Bilkoda, Kirukula, Pannaya) that would resurface, barely renamed, in Chalukya inscriptions a century later. Banavasi's river-and-pass geography had not changed. Its position at the mouth of the Devimane and Arbail routes to the coast had not changed. What changed was only which dynasty's officials collected the toll at the end of the pass.

It is tempting, telling the history of two different dynasties, to treat the second as simply replacing the first — new king, new capital, new chapter, clean break. The evidence resists that framing at almost every point. Kirtivarman I did not build a new trade economy at Banavasi. He inherited one, fully formed, already three or four centuries deep by the time his soldiers arrived, and he kept its administrative language largely intact because there was no reason to reinvent something that already worked. The Chalukyas' genius, if that is the right word for it, was not innovation in how a trading economy should be run. It was patience — first as officials, then as conquerors — in taking over an economy that Chutu customs officers and Kadamba kings had already spent centuries building, and then pointing its accumulated wealth toward a new and much larger architectural experiment eighty kilometres inland, in a town that had never been anyone's capital before.

IX.

The language of the governed and the language of the gods

There is a small, easy-to-miss oddity sitting at the centre of everything written so far. The Aihole inscription — Ravikirti's masterpiece, the single most important document for reconstructing this entire dynasty — is composed in Sanskrit. But it is not written in a Sanskrit script. It is carved in an early form of Kannada script.[37] A Jain poet, praising a Shaivite king, in the prestige language of the north, using the local alphabet of the south. Sit with that combination for a moment, because untangling it — what language people actually spoke, what language they prayed in, what language the state governed in, and what alphabet carried all three — tells you more about how this society actually worked than any single king's biography can.

Start with what predates every dynasty in this story. Kannada is a Dravidian language, one of more than twenty in that family, and it was already old — spoken, argued in, sung, and traded in — long before anyone carved a single letter of it into stone.[38] This is worth stating plainly because the written record can create a false impression: that a language is born the moment it first appears in an inscription. It is not. Colloquial Kannada was the ordinary spoken language of the Malaprabha valley for centuries, perhaps millennia, before the Kadambas or the Chalukyas ever put a chisel to it. What changes across this period is not the existence of the language. It is who decides the language is worth writing down, and for what purpose.

The earliest surviving answer to that question comes not from the Chalukyas at all, but from their predecessors. The Halmidi inscription — a Kadamba-era endowment record from the 5th century, discovered only in 1936 — is the oldest attested Kannada-language inscription anywhere, carved in the earliest form of what would become Kadamba script.[39] It names a king, Kakustha, almost certainly Kakusthavarma, the same Kadamba ruler whose daughters married into the Gupta and Vakataka royal houses a generation earlier. And it names its own engraver: a craftsman called Kavi Kubja, whose signature survives on the stone alongside the record he cut into it — one of the very few named artisans, rather than named kings or poets, that this history has managed to recover.[40] Long before any Chalukya king used Kannada for anything, a Kadamba-era stonecutter was already carving it, competently and confidently, as if the practice were nothing new.

By the Chalukya period, this had become routine practice rather than an isolated experiment. A Badami-era record of Mangalesa, dated 578 CE, survives in Kannada.[41] This matters because Mangalesa was not a minor local official — he was, briefly, king. The dynasty's own royal administration was, from an early point, comfortable committing real governance to the vernacular, not reserving it exclusively for grand dynastic self-portraiture. That grand self-portraiture — the Aihole inscription itself — stayed in Sanskrit, because Sanskrit was the language of pan-Indian prestige, the language a king used when he wanted to be legible to other kings, to scholars, to the deep literary tradition running back through Kalidasa. Kannada, meanwhile, was the language a king used when he wanted to be legible to his own subjects, his own officials, the actual administrative machinery described two sections ago. This is not a contradiction. It is a division of linguistic labour, and it is close to a textbook case of what linguists call diglossia: two languages, or two registers of the same linguistic culture, each doing a different job, coexisting without friction because nobody expected them to do the same job in the first place.

Temple language sat somewhere adjacent to both. By the time of the Chalukyas, Sanskrit had firmly displaced the Prakrit that once dominated religious inscription in this region — the very Prakrit that recorded Sivaskanda Nagasri's Naga-stone gift at Banavasi, three centuries earlier. The Aihole inscription itself opens not with genealogy but with devotion, a salutation to Jinendra, the Jain title for a liberated soul — Sanskrit doing religious work before it does political work, in the very first line.[42] Jain, Shaiva, and Vaishnava dedications across Aihole, Badami, and Pattadakal follow the same pattern: Sanskrit for the sacred, regardless of which sacred tradition was being addressed, in a period of genuine religious pluralism where kings personally devoted to one god funded temples for several others.

What happened next deserves to be called, without exaggeration, a literary event. Around 700 CE, at Badami itself, a record survives attributed to a figure named Kappe Arabhatta, and it is composed not as administrative prose but as verse — the first known Kannada poem, written in tripadi, the three-line metre that would go on to become one of the defining verse forms of the entire Kannada literary tradition.[43] This is the moment Kannada stops being merely a language the state was willing to govern in, and becomes a language literature was being made in — a threshold no less significant, in its own quieter way, than Ravikirti's Sanskrit eulogy on the hill above Aihole. Kannada's own literary self-awareness would not fully crystallise into a surviving critical text until the 9th century, with the Kavirajamarga — a treatise on poetics that explicitly discusses earlier Kannada poets whose actual works have since been lost to time, meaning the tradition Kappe Arabhatta represents was already thick with predecessors and successors we simply cannot read anymore.[44]

Underneath all of this sat the physical alphabet itself, and its own family tree is worth tracing, because it connects every dynasty in this story through a shared visual inheritance even where their politics were openly hostile. The lineage runs from the Ashokan Brahmi script — the same script that carried the emperor's edicts across the subcontinent nine centuries earlier — into a distinct southern variant that scholars now call the Kadamba script, recognisably its own alphabet by the 5th century CE.[45] From there it splits and re-splits: a Badami Chalukya variant in the 6th–7th centuries; a Rashtrakuta variant in the 8th–10th; a later Kalyana Chalukya variant, more cursive and decorative, partly a consequence of being carved into soapstone rather than harder sandstone.[46] The Western Gangas, ruling their own territory alongside and sometimes against the Chalukyas, used a closely related form called Adi Ganga script, visibly descended from the same Kadamba root.[47] Every one of these dynasties — Kadamba, Chalukya, Ganga, and eventually Rashtrakuta — was writing in variations of the same alphabet family, regardless of which of them happened to be at war with which other in a given decade. Political rivalry and shared literacy were not, in this world, in any tension with each other.

That shared alphabet could travel into deeply hostile territory, and one surviving inscription proves it in a single, pointed act. Vikramaditya — one of several Chalukya kings to carry that name, campaigning against the Pallavas — left a Kannada-language inscription inside the Kailasanatha temple at Kanchipuram itself, the Pallava capital.[48] It is difficult to read this as anything other than deliberate: a conqueror's vernacular, carved into the walls of his rival's most sacred building, a small and permanent act of linguistic trespass in the very city whose earlier insult, generations before, had launched the Kadambas' own founder into rebellion in the first place. Language, in this world, was not neutral. Where you carved it, and in what alphabet, was itself a political statement.

It is worth, finally, lifting the frame all the way out, because the seventh century was not only a defining moment for Kannada. It was a defining moment for written language almost everywhere this network of trade and travel touched. Around 650 CE — within a decade or two of Ravikirti finishing the Aihole inscription — the third Caliph, Uthman ibn Affan, commissioned his scribe Zayd ibn Thabit to produce a single, standardised written text of the Quran, in the dialect of the Prophet's own Quraysh tribe, distributing authorised copies to every province of the rapidly expanding Islamic world and ordering all variant versions destroyed.[49] It is close to the exact opposite gesture to what was happening in the Deccan at the same moment: where Arabia was centralising a single sacred text into one enforced standard, Karnataka was doing something more like the reverse, letting a shared script family fragment gently into regional variants — Kadamba, Chalukya, Ganga, Rashtrakuta — without any equivalent drive toward uniformity. Two civilisations, writing down their defining texts in the same generation, made almost opposite choices about what writing was for.

Xuanzang himself, whose visit to Vatapi has already been described at length, spent these same years engaged in his own act of language transfer — translating vast quantities of Sanskrit Buddhist scripture into Chinese, carrying not just relics and manuscripts back toward the Tang court but an entire vocabulary of concepts that Chinese Buddhism would spend the next several centuries digesting.[50] And the Persian court Pulakeshin II had sent his embassy to in 625–626 CE, the Sasanian court of Khusrau II, conducted its own affairs in Pahlavi script — a writing system that, within a single generation of that Ajanta-painted embassy, would be swept away almost entirely, as the Arab conquest of Persia in 651 CE ended Sasanian rule and began Persia's own centuries-long transition toward Arabic script and, eventually, a Persian language written in it.[51] The ambassadors captured mid-gesture on that cave wall belonged to a linguistic world that had, unknown to any of them, less than thirty years left to exist in its old form. Kannada, by contrast — governed in, written down, then sung in verse at Badami around exactly this same moment — was only getting started.

What Kappe Arabhatta began around 700 CE did not stall out after the Chalukyas fell. It accelerated under the dynasty that replaced them. Within a century and a half of that first tripadi verse, the Rashtrakuta king Amoghavarsha I would compose the Kavirajamarga — already mentioned above as the earliest surviving Kannada literary treatise — and the same Rashtrakuta court patronised the three poets later Kannada tradition remembers as its "three gems": Pampa, Ponna, and Ranna, all three, notably, Jain by faith.[51b] Pampa's Vikramarjuna Vijaya and Ranna's later work would give Kannada its first great narrative epics, built on a literary confidence that traces in an unbroken line back through Kappe Arabhatta's single surviving verse to Kavi Kubja's signature on the Halmidi stone. A dynasty that began by absorbing an existing administrative language wholesale ends up, two centuries and one change of ruling house later, presiding over that same language's first true literature. The Chalukyas did not live to see it. The stone they set writing on did.

X.

Silk, stone, and song

History built entirely from kings, taxes, and battles tends to forget that the people living through it also got dressed in the morning, ate something at midday, and had opinions about music. The physical record from Aihole, Badami, and Pattadakal is unusually generous on exactly these points, because so much of it survives not as text but as carved bodies — thousands of them, dancing, standing, adorned, captured in stone by sculptors who were, whether they intended it or not, also documenting how people looked.

The clothing this record shows is consistent and specific. Figures across the temple reliefs — divine and human both — wear a short, draped lower garment, generally secured by an ornamented belt at the waist, its ends falling forward in decorative folds. Above the waist, jewellery does the work clothing does elsewhere: armbands, bangles, elaborate necklaces layered in tiers, large circular earrings stretching the lobe. A relief at Aihole itself shows this in unusually rich detail — a many-armed figure, standing in a relaxed tribhanga pose beside a bull, wearing precisely this combination: a beaded belt with a decorative clasp, multiple necklaces graduated in length, thick armbands on the upper arms, and a lower garment falling in soft verticals to the knee.[52] This was not, historians generally agree, an isolated fashion invented by Chalukya-era sculptors. The convention traces back at least to the Satavahana period — the same centuries covered earlier in this series — meaning the way Aihole's stone bodies are dressed represents genuine continuity in regional taste stretching back through Banavasi's own era, not a fresh invention.

Food is harder to recover in this kind of vivid physical detail, because nobody carved a meal into a temple wall. What survives is closer to agricultural inference: the Malaprabha valley, like the Varada valley around Banavasi before it, supported paddy, wheat, sugarcane, and areca cultivation, the tax base described two sections ago resting directly on this produce.[53] On drink, the record is thinner still, and honesty matters more than colour here: fermented palm toddy and rice-based liquor (sura) were both long-established across the Deccan by this period, appearing in Sanskrit dharma texts as things a Brahmin should abstain from and others might not — but no Chalukya-specific inscription or find has yet turned up a named drink, a tavern, or a drinking scene the way the record so generously supplies dancers and jewellery. It is honest to say the agricultural and legal record gives us the crops and the general social expectation, and leaves the actual glass in anyone's hand to inference rather than evidence.

The social record is a little more forthcoming than the culinary one. Inscriptions from this period name specific women — Vinayavathi and Vijayanka among them — in contexts that suggest real administrative or political standing rather than purely decorative royal mentions, and there is no clear inscriptional evidence of sati in this era despite its later association with the region.[53b] Temples of the period also record the appearance of devadasis, women dedicated to temple service and performance, tying directly back into the dance tradition the Badami Nataraja relief documents in stone.[53c]

Music and dance survive far better, because they too found their way into stone. The most significant single piece of evidence anywhere in this dynasty's territory is a relief in Cave 1 at Badami: an eighteen-armed dancing Shiva, Nataraja, executing what art historians have identified as multiple distinct karanas — formal dance postures — within a single carved figure, each pair of arms frozen in a different stance.[54] This is not decorative flourish. It is close to a technical diagram, a stone-carved reference to a codified vocabulary of movement that would later be systematised in texts like the Natya Shastra tradition, and it stands among the earliest and most complete such records anywhere in Indian sculpture. Whoever commissioned this cave understood dance as something with grammar and structure, precise enough to be worth carving in specific, repeatable detail — not simply "a god dancing," but a god demonstrating. Sage Bharata's Natya Shastra tradition itself was, by this period, in active and advanced development across the Deccan, and inscriptions elsewhere in Chalukya territory independently reference dance and performance in terms that suggest the Badami relief was not an isolated commission but part of a living, working art form.[53d]

Poetry is where this section has to end, because it is where the dynasty's own royal family enters directly rather than through architecture or tax record. Pulakeshin II's own daughter, a poet remembered as Shilabhattarika, wrote in Sanskrit and was regarded, generations later, as significant enough that the ninth-century critic Rajashekhara ranked her among the founders of the Panchali style — a register prized for its careful balance between ornamented language and clear sense — and paired her, as a point of comparison, with Banabhatta, the great prose stylist who had served at the court of Harshavardhana.[55] The comparison is sharper than it first appears. Banabhatta's patron was the very emperor Pulakeshin II defeated at the Narmada; two generations later, critics were placing the victor's daughter's poetry on equal footing with the literary jewel of the loser's court. Only six of Shilabhattarika's poems are known to survive, out of what Rajashekhara implies was a much larger body of work, now lost.[56] She married Dadiga, a prince of the Western Ganga dynasty — Chalukya subordinates, tying literary achievement directly back into the same web of dynastic marriage alliance already seen through Kakusthavarma's daughters two generations earlier. Her son, Mahendravarma, later petitioned a subsequent Chalukya king, Vijayaditya, for a land grant on behalf of a scholar — and it is the copper plates recording that grant, deciphered only in 2023, that finally allowed historians to correctly place Shilabhattarika in the seventh century at all, rather than the eighth-century Rashtrakuta court she had long been mistakenly associated with.[57] Her most famous surviving fragment, on the ache of solitude under moonlight, has outlived the empire that produced her by well over a millennium — reportedly still echoing, transformed, in a twentieth-century Marathi song.[58]

There is something worth naming plainly in the shape of this section as a whole: an eighteen-armed god demonstrating dance technique, a princess writing verse good enough to be compared with her father's greatest rival's own court poet, a belt clasp and a necklace pattern surviving three centuries of continuous use. None of this was made by merchants, and none of it moved along the trade routes we keep returning to. But all of it was paid for by an economy traced across several sections now — the taxes, the tolls, the sea-borne horses, the temple-adjacent wealth of a dynasty whose armies and whose art drew on the same treasury. Culture, here, is not a decoration added after the serious business of conquest and commerce. It is the thing conquest and commerce were, in the end, funding all along.

XI.

The world catches fire

Every part of this story so far has placed the Chalukyas against a wider world in passing — a Persian embassy here, a Chinese pilgrim there. It is worth, once more, stopping to look at that wider world directly, because in the final decades of Chalukya rule, the wider world stopped staying politely in the background and arrived, armed, at the edge of their own territory.

By the early eighth century, the Umayyad Caliphate — the same Islamic empire whose standardisation of the Quran was described earlier, barely two generations before, as a study in centralising contrast with the Deccan's own looser script family — had conquered Sindh under the general Muhammad ibn Qasim, in 712 CE, and stood at the western threshold of the Indian subcontinent for the first time in history.[58b] What followed was not a single invasion but a sustained push: successive Arab governors attempted to extend Umayyad control east and south out of Sindh, into Rajasthan, Malwa, and Gujarat, over the following quarter-century.

The push reached its high-water mark, and broke, in 738 CE. An Umayyad governor named Junayd ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Murri led a large force in two columns out of Sindh — one east toward Malwa, one south into Gujarat.[58c] The eastern column was destroyed near Ujjain by the Gurjara-Pratihara king Nagabhata I. The southern column, moving into Gujarat, was met and defeated at Navsari — and the man who defeated it was Avanijanashraya Pulakeshi, a Chalukya governor, sent specifically for this purpose by the Badami Chalukya king Vikramaditya II.[58d] It is a genuinely remarkable fact, easy to lose among the larger sweep of this story: a Chalukya army, operating on the Caliphate's own frontier, hundreds of kilometres from Vatapi, was directly responsible for stopping the Umayyad Caliphate's eastward expansion into India. Arab chroniclers themselves record the defeat plainly; the Caliph Mahdi is later quoted, by Arab sources, as having given up on the project of conquering any further part of India at all.[58e]

Dantidurga — whose fuller story comes next — was, at the time of this campaign, still a Chalukya feudatory rather than the founder of a rival dynasty. Historical summaries of his early career place him assisting the Chalukyas against these same Arab incursions in the years around 731 to 739 CE, meaning the very man who would end Chalukya rule in 753 CE had, less than fifteen years earlier, fought to preserve it against a force that had already toppled the Sasanian Empire, absorbed the whole of Persia, and reached, by then, from the Atlantic coast of Iberia to the Indus.[58f] The Chalukyas' own end, in other words, came from within a political world that had, within living memory, helped repel the single most expansive military power on the planet.

Cast the frame wider still, and the same decades look no less dramatic anywhere else this story has touched. In China, the Tang dynasty — the same imperial house Xuanzang had once served, and the same one whose court his translated Buddhist texts were still being studied in — was, through the early-to-mid eighth century, at the height of its own cosmopolitan power, its capital Chang'an arguably the largest and most international city on Earth, receiving traders, monks, and diplomats from Persia, Central Asia, and beyond.[58g] In Byzantium, the Eastern Roman Empire — heir, however distantly, to the very Rome whose coin moulds and bullion were traced arriving at Banavasi — had itself only recently survived an Umayyad siege of Constantinople (717–718 CE), turning back the same expansionist wave that would, two decades later, be stopped again at Navsari by a Chalukya governor most world histories have never heard of.[58h] Three unconnected armies, on three different continents, within a single generation, each independently checking the same Arab expansion at its outer edge — Constantinople in the west, Navsari and Avanti in the south Asian theatre — while, in between, the Abbasid Revolution was already brewing that would, within a decade of Dantidurga's own conquest of Vatapi, topple the Umayyads entirely and shift the Islamic world's centre of gravity from Damascus to Baghdad.[58i] The Chalukyas' fall, seen this way, is not an isolated Deccan story. It is one small, specific instance of an entire mid-eighth-century world simultaneously absorbing shocks from every direction at once — a genuinely global moment of upheaval, of which Kirtivarman II's defeat at Dantidurga's hands is a minor, local, and in context almost coincidental echo.

XII.

The face that finally arrives

Every dynasty in this story falls to the next one, and the Chalukyas of Badami were no exception. In 753 CE, Kirtivarman II — the last Chalukya to rule from Vatapi — was defeated by Dantidurga, a vassal who had served the Chalukyas the way the Chalukyas themselves had once served the Kadambas.[59] It is, once again, the same pattern this history keeps circling back to: not an outside conqueror arriving to smash an unfamiliar system, but a trusted subordinate who understood the machinery from the inside, judged the moment right, and took it over rather than tearing it down. And as the previous section made clear, Dantidurga's path to that moment ran directly through the Arab wars: a feudatory who had spent the 730s helping defend Chalukya territory against the Umayyad advance turned, within about fifteen years, into the man who ended Chalukya rule entirely — the same military competence the dynasty had once relied on eventually being turned against it.

Dantidurga did not simply defeat Kirtivarman II and stop there. His own inscriptions, read with appropriate scepticism for the genre's habitual exaggeration, claim campaigns reaching from the Mahi river in Gujarat to the Mahanadi in Kalinga — victories over the Gurjaras of Malwa, the Chalukyas of Lata, and rulers as far afield as Kosala and Kanchi.[59b] More reliable than the geography is the theatre. Around 752-753 CE, at Ujjain — a genuinely ancient seat of imperial legitimacy, chosen deliberately — Dantidurga performed the Hiranyagarbha ("golden womb") ritual, a Vedic ceremony understood to symbolically rebirth its patron as a Kshatriya, cleansing away any question of a feudatory family's lesser origin and replacing it with unambiguous royal sanction.[59c] The Samangad copper plates record that the Gurjara king of Malwa attended this ceremony not as an equal but in the specific ritual role of pratihara — doorkeeper — a detail that reads as a pointed, deliberate pun aimed squarely at the Gurjara-Pratihara dynasty's own name, staged for an audience that would certainly have caught it.[59d] It is worth remembering that this same Gurjara-Pratihara line, under Nagabhata I, had helped stop the Arab advance at Avanti barely fifteen years earlier — meaning Dantidurga's theatrical humiliation of a Gurjara king at Ujjain was aimed at a dynasty that had, within living memory, fought on the very same side he had.

One correction is worth making plainly, since popular accounts routinely get it wrong: Dantidurga did not build the Kailasa temple at Ellora. The Archaeological Survey of India is explicit that the only securely dated Dantidurga inscription at Ellora sits in Cave 15, the Dashavatara cave — not Cave 16, the Kailasa, which belongs unambiguously to his uncle and successor, Krishna I.[59e] What Dantidurga leaves at Ellora is a threshold rather than a monument: the first securely epigraphic Rashtrakuta presence at a site that would, within a generation, host one of the greatest single works of rock-cut architecture anywhere on Earth — modelled, as already noted, directly on the Chalukyas' own Virupaksha temple at Pattadakal. Dantidurga died without a male heir, and the throne passed to Krishna I, who would take the conception this series has traced from Aihole's earliest experiments all the way through to its final, mountain-sized expression.[59f] Dantidurga founded the Rashtrakuta dynasty, and moved the seat of power to a new capital, Manyakheta — a city, and a chapter, left for what comes next.

The Rashtrakutas did to the Chalukya inheritance exactly what the Chalukyas had done to the Kadamba one. They kept the tax categories. They kept the administrative hierarchy, the Vishaya-Bhoga-Grama chain, the merchant-and-banker councils sitting alongside district officials. And Krishna I's Kailasa, as already noted, was Pattadakal's Virupaksha temple reborn at mountain scale.[60] Conquest, once again, absorbed rather than erased what it defeated. A Rashtrakuta king's proudest monument was, in its bones, a Chalukya design.

But there is one more thread to follow before this part of the story can properly end, and it is the thread deliberately withheld since Section IV. There, describing the coins and the sculptors and the naval reach that prove Chalukya-era wealth without quite proving who held it, this account admitted something plainly: unlike Banavasi, which had its Bhutapala — a named merchant, a real inscription, a face — this era had not yet produced its equivalent. The promise, at the time, was that this face would arrive eventually. It arrives now, and it arrives in a genuinely different shape than expected.

Somewhere between roughly 700 and 730 CE — while Kappe Arabhatta's tripadi verse was being carved at Badami, while Vikramaditya I and his successors were still ruling from Vatapi — an inscription was cut at the Durga Temple in Aihole, the very apsidal structure already dwelt on for its unusual chariot-shaped plan.[61] It records the temple's patron: a man named Komarasinga, described as possibly a leader of what the inscription calls the Aihole Five Hundred — an early, local artisan and merchant assembly, honouring the sun god Surya. This is not Bhutapala. There is no dramatic Prakrit boast about being "the most excellent in Jambudvipa," no evidence of the kind of individual wealth that funded a monument hundreds of kilometres from home. What it offers instead is something arguably more important: the earliest physical trace of an organisation that would, within a few centuries, call itself the Ayyavole 500 — a name that is simply Aihole's own Kannada name, Ayyavole, attached to the number that had, by then, already become a kind of brand.

Everything assembled across this account points toward exactly this outcome, once it is laid out end to end. The mahallakas — the chief merchant, the chief banker, the head of the artisans' guild — who sat inside Chalukya district government with "a large voice in administration," described several sections ago, are not a separate story from Komarasinga's guild. They are its immediate institutional ancestor: merchants who were not petitioning power from outside, but who had already been formally embedded in local governance for generations by the time anyone thought to give their collective identity a name and carve it into a temple wall. The Sandhagaram — the roofed, wall-less assembly platform used for communal and administrative business at these same temple complexes — is very plausibly where such a guild's own councils met, discussed trade regulations, and settled disputes, long before any of it survives as text. And the deep continuity traced twice already — Jayasimha and Ranaraga rising through Kadamba administration before their descendants took the throne; Kirtivarman I inheriting Banavasi's tax categories rather than reinventing them — is, in the end, the same shape of story as a guild growing out of a workshop town's existing merchant and artisan population, rather than arriving from elsewhere to claim it.

There is no single grand founder to name here, and that absence is itself the point, not a gap in the record. Unlike a king, whose power by definition concentrates in one body, a merchant guild's power was always going to be distributed — a "collection of franchises cooperating under a shared name, with no single leader and no head office," in the words later used to describe this same organisation at the height of its Chola-era power, centuries after Komarasinga's temple was finished.[62] The Ainnurruvar was never going to have a Bhutapala, a single dazzling individual whose wealth could stand in for the whole. It was, from its very beginnings at Aihole, built to be something else: durable specifically because it did not depend on any one man's fortune, capable of outlasting Kadambas, Chalukyas, and Rashtrakutas alike, because its foundation was never a king's favour but a workshop town's own accumulated skill, capital, and self-governance, quietly formalising itself while kings were still busy fighting over Vatapi.

The dynasty that opened this part of the story with an administrative family's slow climb from Kadamba service to Chalukya sovereignty closes it by watching a different kind of institution complete the same climb — not toward a throne, but toward something that would outlive every throne in this story. Aihole gave its name to a temple, gave its craftsmen to two centuries of architectural experiment, gave its administrative apparatus to a merchant council that eventually needed no king's court to survive in. The passes and rivers this series opened with, controlling Banavasi's trade, are still there. The taxes, renamed twice already across two centuries, are still being collected. And somewhere in a temple built for the sun god, a workshop town's own merchants have just begun writing themselves into stone — five hundred strong, in name if not yet in fact, on their way to becoming the organisation the next part of this series will follow across an ocean.

Karnataka Trade Cities — Essay I of IV

Water, Passes,
and Gold

The making of Banavasi — how a river-town with no coastline became one of the wealthiest crossings in the Deccan.

↓ scroll
— Ram Devagiri
I.

The stone that survives

There is a stone at Banavasi that has been standing for roughly eighteen hundred years, and it does not commemorate a king. It shows a serpent with five hoods, carved in the style the region reserved for its oldest and most local gods, and beside it runs an inscription in Prakrit recording the gift of a princess.

Her name was Sivaskanda Nagasri, daughter of a minor dynast named Haritiputra Vinhukada Chutukulananda Satakarni, and in the twelfth year of her father's reign — sometime in the later second century CE — she paid for a Buddhist monastery, a water tank, and this very stone, and had her act of generosity carved into rock so that it would outlast her.[1] It has. The stone still sits within the precincts of the Madhukeshwara temple today, long after the dynasty that produced her has been reduced to a paragraph in gazetteers, long after the empire that eventually swallowed her father's kingdom has itself vanished, long after the language she spoke has changed beyond recognition.

It is worth starting here, rather than with the more famous dynasty that later made this town its capital, because the stone makes an argument that the rest of this history bears out again and again: Banavasi's importance did not begin with the people who are remembered for ruling it. It began with the water around it, and the hills behind it, and with what could be moved between the two.

To understand why an inland town in the hill country of Karnataka became one of the wealthiest and most fought-over settlements in the peninsula, you have to stop thinking about the Indian Ocean trade as something that happened at ports, and start thinking about it as something that happened along lines — lines of passes, riverbanks, and cart-tracks, each one a narrow throat through which the wealth of an ocean had to be squeezed before it could reach anyone inland. Banavasi sat on one of those throats. It was never a port. It was, more usefully, a valve.

II.

The town before the dynasty

By the time anyone we might call a "king of Banavasi" existed in any organised sense, the town had already been a place of consequence for centuries. The Varada River wraps around it on three sides, giving it what a much later court poet — writing for the Chalukya emperor Pulakeshin II, who came to know the town as a conquest rather than a birthright — called a jala-durga, a water-fort.[2] Long before any court poet wrote anything, the site had already produced Mesolithic tools and Iron Age megalithic burials, the debris of people who had worked out, without writing any of it down, that a loop of river and a gap in the hills made a good place to stop.[3]

The written record begins to thicken in the third century BCE, when the Sri Lankan chronicle known as the Mahavamsa records that the emperor Ashoka, in the course of dispatching Buddhist missionaries across his empire and beyond it, sent a monk named Rakshita to a place identified with this region.[4] It passed afterward under the loose umbrella of Satavahana authority, and then more directly under a local Satavahana offshoot called the Chutus, whose rulers governed from Banavasi itself between roughly 125 and 345 CE — the dynasty into which Sivaskanda Nagasri, our princess with the serpent stone, was born.[5]

It was during this Chutu period, not the later and more celebrated Kadamba one, that Banavasi acquired its most striking piece of external corroboration. Around 150 CE, the Alexandrian geographer Claudius Ptolemy compiled his Geography, an attempt to fix the coordinates of the known world as reported to him by merchants, sailors, and earlier geographers he trusted. Among the towns of the interior Deccan that made his list is one he renders as "Banaouasei."[6]

Somebody, in other words, thought Banavasi worth mentioning to a Greek.

The town wore several names across the centuries that followed — Vaijayanti in Sanskrit court poetry, Jayantipura once the Kadambas made it their seat, and later Konkanapura in the account of a Chinese pilgrim we will return to — and each name marks a different regime laying its own vocabulary over the same strategic patch of ground.[7]

III.

Why an inland town controlled the sea's wealth

The obvious question is why any of this mattered, given that Banavasi sits some eighty kilometres from the Arabian Sea, with a mountain range in between. The answer is the mountain range itself. The Western Ghats do not simply separate the Karnataka plateau from its coast; they force anyone moving goods between the two through a small number of viable passes, and the passes nearest Banavasi — the routes that modern roads still trace today through Devimane and Arbail towards Kumta — are the ones that geography, not any king, selected as the crossing points.[8] A town that sat athwart the interior mouth of those passes did not need to produce anything itself to become rich. It needed only to be unavoidable.

This is worth dwelling on because it inverts the usual picture of ancient trade, which tends to fixate on the glamour of the coast — the wharves of Muziris, the Roman amphorae, the Yavana merchants disembarking with wine and glassware. Those ports were real and they mattered enormously. But wine and glass and Roman gold did not simply appear in the bazaars of the Deccan interior by magic; they had to be carried, on pack animals and ox-cart, up through a pass, past a town that could tax them, garrison the route, and offer safe lodging for the caravan before it pressed on toward Paithan or the wider Deccan. Banavasi's river, meanwhile, did the humbler but no less essential work of moving goods locally once they were off the mountain track.[9] The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, the extraordinary first-century merchant's handbook that survives from a Greek-speaking sailor working these waters, describes pepper above all moving out, and wine, glass, and worked metal moving in.[10] Banavasi's fortune was to sit on the road that pepper had to walk before it became a caravan's cargo rather than a farmer's harvest.

IV.

What the coins and the stones prove

Abstractions about "trade routes" are cheap; what makes this history persuasive is the physical evidence, and Banavasi has produced an unusual amount of it for a town its size. Excavations and coin hoards at the site and nearby have turned up punch-marked silver coins of the old pan-Indian type, lead coinage issued locally under Satavahana rulers such as Yajna Sri Satakarni, and — more startling — a mould for casting Roman coins bearing the design of the emperor Tiberius, alongside fragments of fine Arretine pottery imported from Italy and locally made Rouletted Ware influenced by Mediterranean forms.[11] None of this proves that Roman merchants themselves walked Banavasi's streets. It proves something arguably more interesting: that Roman-derived wealth had penetrated far enough inland to be locally recast and locally desired, two hundred kilometres from the nearest wharf.

If any single piece of evidence makes the abstraction concrete, though, it is not found at Banavasi at all, but four hundred kilometres to the north, cut into a rock face at Karla, near modern Pune. There, on the veranda of the largest rock-cut Buddhist prayer hall in South Asia, a Prakrit inscription records a gift. It reads, in translation:

"Seth Bhutapala, from Vejayanti, has established this rock mansion, the most excellent in Jambudvipa."[12]

Setthi is the term — the root of the modern Indian "seth" — for a guild-master, banker, or wealthy merchant, and Vejayanti is Vaijayanti, the very name by which Banavasi was known in this period. A merchant from this inland Karnataka town had, by the first century CE, accumulated enough capital to fund the completion of one of the great religious monuments of the age, in a city hundreds of kilometres from his own, alongside Yavana — Greek and West Asian — donors making gifts of their own.[13]

V.

A Brahmin's rebellion, and a dynasty

Banavasi's political fortunes changed decisively in the mid-fourth century CE, and the story, as the Kadambas themselves chose to tell it in the Talagunda pillar inscription — composed around 450 CE and the most trusted early source for the dynasty's founding — has the shape of a grievance nursed into a kingdom.[14] A Brahmin named Mayurasharma, born at Talagunda not far from Banavasi, had travelled to Kanchipuram, the great Pallava capital, to complete his Vedic studies at a ghatika — a religious college where, by one near-contemporary description, examination itself was a kind of lottery: passages of scripture were copied onto palm-leaf slips and sealed into a pot, and a candidate's fate at the hands of his examiners depended on which slip he happened to draw.[15] There, by the inscription's account, a Pallava horseman insulted him — aimed at his caste rather than his person. Mayurasharma did not return to his books. He abandoned scholarship for the sword, gathered the support of forest peoples along the Karnataka frontier, and fought a guerrilla campaign against Pallava frontier forces until the Pallavas, unable to crush him outright, chose instead to recognise him.[16]

Mayurasharma made Banavasi his capital, inheriting a town whose trade infrastructure, coinage, and regional prestige had already been built by dynasties before him, and grafted a new royal lineage onto old commercial foundations.[17]

His successors did not squander the position. Kakusthavarma, who ruled in the first half of the fifth century, pursued a strategy of dynastic marriage so successful that his daughters married into the Gupta and Vakataka royal houses — meaning that within roughly a century of its founding on the strength of a caste insult and a forest rebellion, this Deccan hill-town dynasty was intermarrying with the most powerful empire in North India.[18] Ravivarma, who followed, extended the kingdom's reach north to the Narmada and cultivated a court remembered for its patronage of dance, music, and drama.[19]

VI.

The examined life

The grievance that made Mayurasharma a king began, not incidentally, in a classroom — and it is worth pausing on the world of learning, food, and dress that the Kadambas built once they had one of their own to rule. The family's very first recorded act, well before Mayurasharma took up arms, was scholarly rather than military: an ancestor named Mukanna had already brought thirty-two learned Brahmins from Ahichchhatra in the north and settled them at Talagunda around 350 CE, founding what is now considered the earliest known agrahara — a resident scholarly community — anywhere in Karnataka.[20] It endured astonishingly well: an inscription from as late as 1150 CE shows the same institution still running, eight centuries on, with forty-eight students and eight teachers dividing their time between the Vedas, grammar, logic, and drama, and — in a detail that says as much about daily life as any coin hoard — with specific provision made for the clothing of students and teachers alike, along with cooks retained to feed the whole resident community.[21] A curriculum, a wardrobe, and a kitchen, all endowed by the state in one act: it is about as close as the period gets to a welfare institution.

Governing the kingdom that paid for this took its own kind of expertise. Inscriptions preserve a small bureaucratic vocabulary — the mahattara (village elder), the vishayapati (district governor), the manevergade (household or revenue officer), the tantrapala (a keeper of administrative method) — evidence of a state specialised enough to need different words for different kinds of official, rather than treating governance as a single undifferentiated task.[22]

Fed by the same Varada silt that made the town defensible, the surrounding valley grew paddy, wheat, sugarcane, arecanut, and spices — a diet and a trade good in the same crop, since pepper and other spices moving through Banavasi's markets were as likely to end up in a local kitchen as in a caravan bound for the Ghats.[23] The temple that still stands at the town's centre carries this appetite in its very name: Madhukeshwara, "the lord of honey," so called for the honey-coloured stone of its central lingam — a small but telling instance of a place naming its holiest object after something it grew.

Craft, too, left its mark in stone. Kadamba-era temple builders made a signature of the perforated screen window — a lattice of pierced stone cut to filter light and air into a shrine's interior without admitting rain or direct sun, a modest piece of structural engineering that later Deccan dynasties would inherit and refine.[24]

VII.

Life along the river

Beyond the classroom and the kitchen, the inscriptions describe a wider society organised in nested administrative units — districts called vishaya, subdivided into smaller mahagrama and dashagrama groupings — and an economy resting on the twin pillars of grain and cattle, worked by a landholding peasant class known as the Gavundas.[25] Trade itself was itemised and taxed with a precision that tells its own story: a transit levy called perjunka on goods in motion, a sales tax called bilkoda, a land tax called kirukula, and even a specific duty, pannaya, on betel leaf.[26]

The region's most distinctive commemorative practice, the hero stone or virgal — carved memorials to warriors who died defending cattle or territory, of which more than 2,650 survive from this era in Karnataka alone — testifies to a martial economy built around the protection of livestock and grazing land as much as around trade goods.[27] Religiously, the picture was genuinely plural: the Kadambas patronised Shaivism as a family cult, yet funded Jain basadis and Buddhist viharas alongside it, while terracotta folk figurines and inscribed Naga stones — cousins of Sivaskanda Nagasri's serpent — show older worship persisting beneath the newer state religions.[28] The Kadambas are also credited with originating a distinctive regional temple form, the stepped, pyramidal Kadamba Shikara tower, whose oldest surviving example stands at Halasi.[29]

And in the inscriptions themselves, language quietly performs its own revolution. The earliest Kadamba records are in Sanskrit and Prakrit, following convention; but by the mid-fifth century, Kannada begins to surface in the epigraphic record — most famously in the Halmidi inscription of around 450 CE, with an even earlier claimant, the Talagunda lion balustrade inscription dated to 370 CE, now argued by some scholars to be the oldest surviving Kannada inscription of all.[30] A dynasty founded on a Sanskrit-speaking Brahmin's grievance ended up presiding over the moment a regional vernacular first claimed the permanence of stone.

VIII.

Afterward

The Kadambas did not last, in the way that no dynasty in this story ultimately lasts. Around 540 CE, weakened by succession disputes that had already seen one ruler murdered by a rival branch of his own family, the kingdom was absorbed by the rising power of the Chalukyas of Badami.[31] Individual Kadamba lineages persisted for centuries afterward as feudatories in Goa, Hangal, and Halasi — demoted from sovereigns to subordinates, but not erased.[32] A Chinese pilgrim named Xuanzang, travelling through the peninsula around 640 CE, recorded visiting a kingdom he called Konkanapura, describing its magnificent stupas and monasteries — though the identification of this Konkanapura with Banavasi is disputed among historians, one proposal among several competing candidates, not a settled fact.[33]

What did outlast the dynasty, more reliably than any single ruler's memory, was the town's function. Long after the Kadambas were a subordinate footnote to someone else's empire, Banavasi's position at the mouth of the Ghats and on the banks of the Varada continued to matter to whoever ruled the region next — because the mountains had not moved, and the river had not moved, and the passes that a Chutu customs officer and a Kadamba tax-collector had both taxed in their turn were still, centuries later, the only sensible way to get a caravan from the coast to the interior.

Dynasties rise and are absorbed. Geography, and the wealth that geography compels to move along fixed lines, proves considerably more patient.

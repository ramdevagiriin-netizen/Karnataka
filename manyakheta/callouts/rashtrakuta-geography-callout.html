Callout — Geography & Extent

One Empire,
Six Modern States

The Rashtrakuta empire, at its widest reach, spanned territory that today belongs to six separate Indian states — plus its ports, its ambitions, and the mountain passes that made moving anything between coast and capital possible at all.

The map below is a schematic, not a survey — built from approximate coordinates, not period cartography (none survives) or modern GIS boundary data. Its purpose is orientation: to show, at a glance, how much of what is now separately governed as Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu once answered, in some form, to a single capital at Manyakheta.

Reading the shaded zone

Not a border, a reach

Early medieval Deccan polities didn't hold territory the way a modern state does — the shaded area marks the zone of fairly consistent Rashtrakuta administration and tribute, not a hard, patrolled line. Vengi, on the eastern edge, spent long stretches as a nominally submissive but practically independent Chalukya branch; the Gangas of southern Karnataka were feudatories, governing their own affairs under Rashtrakuta overlordship rather than direct rule.

Reading the reach-points

Campaigns, not conquests

Kannauj, Kanchi, and Rameshwaram were never incorporated — they were reached, fought over, and in Kannauj's case briefly occupied, before the army marched home. The dashed lines mark campaign distance from Manyakheta, not administrative control.

Known

Sopara (Sūrpāraka)

The great western port, named directly in the Kuvalayamalakaha's eight-merchant scene and in Arab geographical accounts. Identification is secure.

Known

Thana & Chaul

Named repeatedly by Arab writers across the ninth and tenth centuries as wealthy, well-established Konkan ports with settled Muslim trading communities.

Inferred

Sindabur / Goa

Arab-writer "Sindabur" is generally identified with the Goa region, but the identification rests on phonetic and geographic inference, not a confirmed inscription naming the same place under both names.

Inferred

Naoura / Honnavar

The Periplus's first-century "Naoura" is scholarly consensus-identified with modern Honnavar — a plausible, widely accepted, but still inferential match, centuries before this book's own period.

Known

Thal & Bhor Ghats

Historically significant passes linking the Konkan coast to the Deccan plateau — the same structural role Devimane and Arbail played further south for Banavasi's own trade in an earlier century.

Known

Devimane & Arbail

The passes already established in this account's own opening chapters, carrying trade from the Konkan coast up to Banavasi — still functionally relevant to the wider network Manyakheta later inherited.
